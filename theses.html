Research Focus

My academic essays focus on three primary areas:

  1. Policy Analysis & Causal Inference - Applying quasi-experimental methods (RDD, DiD) to evaluate real-world policies and quantify causal effects, with a focus on communicating results and limitations to inform decision-making
  2. Distributional Policy Analysis - Examining how policies affect different segments of the population, with attention to those who are worse-off and the philosophical foundations of Prioritarianism
  3. Social Preferences - Using experimental research designs to measure behavioural outcomes such as altruism and self-defeating behaviour

MSc Thesis in Behavioural Science

Distribution Sensitive Policy Analysis based on WELLBYs: Constructing a Prioritarian Social Welfare Function through Causal Evidence on Life Satisfaction

MSc Dissertation | Behavioural Science | London School of Economics and Political Science | 2024 | Grade: 65%

Supervised by Dr. Christian Krekel

Difference-in-Differences Panel Data Analysis Propensity Score Matching Stata Welfare Economics Distributional Analysis Policy Evaluation

Abstract

This dissertation is the first study to construct a Prioritarian Social Welfare Function (SWF) using life satisfaction (WELLBYs) as a welfare measure. It begins with a comprehensive theoretical analysis justifying Prioritarianism — the view that improvements for those worse-off carry greater moral weight — over Utilitarianism and Egalitarianism, drawing on claims-based fairness and the levelling-down objection. Life satisfaction is justified as the welfare measure due to its alignment with democratic principles and psychometric validity.

The empirical core applies a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) design to Australia's major household panel survey (HILDA) to isolate the causal effect of Victoria's second COVID-19 lockdown (July–October 2020) on life satisfaction. The treatment group (Victoria, August–November 2020) is compared to the control group (all other Australian states) across time. Propensity Score Matching and parallel trends verification are used to strengthen identification.

Results show a highly significant negative effect of −0.0303 points on a 0–10 scale (95% CI −0.0486 to −0.0120, p = 0.006), with disproportionately large effects on individuals with lower baseline life satisfaction, poor mental health, and younger age (15–19 years).

The DiD estimates are then fed into the Atkinson SWF to produce distributional welfare comparisons: the lockdown scenario yields 13,775,625 Prioritarian WELLBYs versus 52,366,212 under Business-as-Usual. The analysis concludes that the wellbeing decline from the lockdown outweighed the benefits of reduced premature deaths — offering a novel, distribution-sensitive framework for evaluating public health policy.

Key Contributions:

  • Novel methodology: first application of a Prioritarian SWF using life satisfaction (WELLBYs) as a welfare measure, bridging philosophical theory and empirical policy analysis
  • Causal identification: Difference-in-Differences design on HILDA panel data with Propensity Score Matching and parallel trends testing to isolate the lockdown effect
  • Distributional insight: heterogeneous effects analysis revealing that the policy disproportionately harmed the worse-off — those with low life satisfaction, poor mental health, and adolescents
  • Welfare accounting: quantified social welfare under lockdown vs. Business-as-Usual using the Atkinson SWF, translating causal estimates into actionable policy recommendations

Philosophy of Economics

Well-Being is Measurable After All: A Case for the Differential Realization View

Essay | Philosophy of Economics | London School of Economics and Political Science | 2024 | Grade: 69%

Well-Being Measurement Psychometrics Welfare Economics Philosophy of Science Policy Metrics

Abstract

This essay engages a foundational debate in welfare economics: whether well-being can be validly measured for policy purposes. Hausman (2006) argues it cannot — that the natural heterogeneity of well-being across individuals and circumstances makes any measurement instrument incapable of enabling meaningful cross-individual comparisons. Alexandrova (2013, 2016) offers an optimistic counterargument, proposing that heterogeneity is manageable through modest generalisation — defining well-being for specific kinds of people in specific circumstances — and construct validity of psychometric scales.

The essay endorses Alexandrova's optimism but reopens the debate between her preferred Contextualism and the Differential Realization (DR) view. Contextualism holds that the semantic content of well-being varies with context, making a uniform concept impossible. The DR view argues instead for an invariant semantic core — well-being's meaning stays constant — while contextual variation is captured through the truth conditions that determine when a well-being claim holds in a given situation.

The essay challenges Alexandrova's two premises for Contextualism: first, that well-being should remain stable across contexts (analogous to Epistemic Contextualism) — I argue well-being is inherently sensitive to situational factors, making this analogy invalid. Second, that an invariant core is insufficient to capture well-being's full complexity — I argue it is both sufficient and preferable, as a consistent semantic standard enables cross-contextual policy comparisons without the measurement fragmentation Contextualism would require.

The essay concludes that the DR view with life satisfaction as an invariant concept is the superior foundation for policy-relevant well-being measurement: it preserves comparability across populations while remaining sensitive to contextual determinants through variable truth conditions.

Key Contributions:

  • Philosophical argument: original case for the Differential Realization view over Contextualism, directly challenging Alexandrova's two premises with reasoned counter-arguments
  • Policy relevance: demonstrates why a stable, invariant well-being concept is essential for coherent cross-contextual policy evaluation and comparison
  • Measurement foundations: provides the philosophical grounding for using life satisfaction as a welfare metric in empirical policy analysis — directly informing the approach taken in the MSc dissertation

Behavioural Science Theory

A Dual-Process Model of Self-Defeating Behaviours

Essay | Fundamentals of Behavioural Science | London School of Economics and Political Science | 2023 | Grade: 73%

Dual-Process Theory Behavioural Economics Nudge Theory Intervention Design Policy Implications

Abstract

This essay applies dual-process theory (Evans & Stanovich, 2013; Kahneman & Frederick, 2002) to analyse the cognitive mechanisms behind self-defeating behaviours — defined as repetitive actions that invite failure and prevent individuals from attaining their goals. It builds a theoretical framework around two root causes: self-control failures (the tension between the impulsive "doer" and the forward-looking "planner", Thaler & Shefrin, 1981) and negatively biased self-reflection (System 1 pattern-completion that reinforces pessimistic self-associations and drives avoidance behaviour).

The essay applies this framework to concrete behavioural domains — overspending, substance abuse, binge-eating, and task avoidance driven by low self-esteem — explaining how limited working memory capacity constrains System 2's ability to intervene and correct automatic biases (Gunn & Finn, 2015; Beevers, 2005).

Drawing on this analysis, the essay proposes and evaluates a typology of cost-effective policy interventions: other-deployed approaches (defaults such as automatic pension enrolment, Madrian & Shea, 2001; taxes on addictive goods, Gruber & Mullainathan, 2006) that reduce reliance on reflective processing, and self-deployed strategies (mindfulness meditation, mental contrasting) that build System 2 capacity and agency.

The essay concludes with an ethical analysis of the autonomy trade-off: self-deployed interventions enhance agency by strengthening System 2, while other-deployed paternalistic measures reduce autonomy by targeting System 1 — yet may be socially optimal in the presence of externalities or where System 2 intervention is not feasible (e.g. severe addiction).

Key Contributions:

  • Theoretical framework: original classification of self-defeating behaviours into self-control failures and negatively biased self-reflection, analysed through a dual-process lens
  • Intervention typology: structured comparison of other-deployed vs. self-deployed policy approaches, grounded in empirical evidence from behavioural economics
  • Ethical reasoning: systematic analysis of the autonomy–welfare trade-off in paternalistic policy design, relevant for real-world nudge and public health applications
  • Literature synthesis: integrates cognitive science, behavioural economics, and public policy literatures to produce actionable design principles for behaviour change interventions

Policy Analysis & Causal Inference

The Effect of Cash Transfers on Political Support

Policy Analysis Essay | Quantitative Methods | London School of Economics and Political Science | 2024 | Grade: 83%

Replication and examination of Manacorda, Miguel, & Vigorito (2011), American Economic Journal: Applied Economics

Regression Discontinuity Design Causal Inference Stata Quasi-Experimental Methods Policy Evaluation Robustness Testing

Abstract

This essay investigates the causal effect of governmental cash and food card transfers on self-reported political support for the incumbent government in Uruguay. Replicating and extending Manacorda et al. (2011), the analysis applies a Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) — a quasi-experimental method exploiting a sharp eligibility cutoff in a predicted income score to compare treatment and control groups around the threshold.

The preliminary analysis uses McCrary density tests (p = 0.4111) and placebo regressions on covariates to verify the key RDD assumptions of no manipulation and local randomization around the cutoff.

Main results show a consistent positive treatment effect of 9–13% on government support in 2007 and 2008, with significant positive effects on confidence in the president, confidence in the Ministry of Social Development (MIDES), and interest in politics.

A comprehensive suite of robustness checks — including donut hole regressions (excluding observations closest to the cutoff), bandwidth sensitivity analyses, and local polynomial models — confirm the stability and significance of the treatment effect across specifications.

The essay concludes with a structured policy critique: limitations in external validity (local average treatment effect for low-income earners only), potential internal validity concerns (contact bias from MIDES personnel), and suggested extensions using Difference-in-Differences and Instrumental Variables designs to improve identification.

Key Analytical Contributions:

  • Full RDD pipeline in Stata: data preparation, McCrary test, placebo regressions on covariates, OLS main regressions, and graphical visualisations
  • Robustness checks: donut hole regressions across multiple exclusion radii, bandwidth sensitivity plots, and local polynomial (quadratic/cubic) models
  • Policy critique: structured assessment of methodological and contextual limitations with concrete suggestions for alternative causal designs (DiD, IV)
  • Replication of a published AEJ paper, demonstrating ability to work with real-world survey data and reproduce peer-reviewed empirical findings
